How student groups operate

Student groups give business students real experience beyond the classroom. Events, speakers, trainings, and client projects are all ways to do that.

Leading a student group comes with responsibility. The work has two purposes, and they carry equal weight:

Protect the school

Avoid mistakes that harm the School of Business, the university, or the people we work with.

Let students flourish

Set students up to succeed, build real skills, and protect their own reputations.

The further a group reaches outside the business school, the more is at stake for both. This framework scales planning and oversight to match.

It comes from experience. Over several years of student groups bringing in speakers and doing client work, most problems traced back to the same few causes: acting before planning, unclear roles, and work the advisor didn't know about. A group that learns from that history can last. One that ignores it usually doesn't.

Freedom is earned by showing a plan. Plans don't need to be perfect. They need to show you thought it through before acting.

Process

Pick your tier

Every activity moves through the same five steps. What changes by tier is how much the plan has to cover.

  1. Pick the tier

    Decide which tier the activity falls in. If it touches two, use the higher one.

  2. Write the plan

    Cover what that tier asks for. Keep it short and clear.

  3. Get approval

    The advisor reviews it and routes it on when the tier requires.

  4. Run it

    Work inside the approved plan. Anything outside it comes back to the advisor.

  5. Close it out

    Record what happened and what you learned, so the next team starts ahead.

Tier 1 · Internal

Students working with students. This is where most of a group's work with the wider student body happens, including events the School of Business relies on.

Your plan covers

  • A semester calendar of meetings, workshops, and events
  • Who leads each one
  • Rooms and resources you'll need

Checks

  • Advisor sees the calendar at the start of the semester
  • Room and resource requests go through normal school channels
  • Any promotion follows the social and branding standards

Tier 2 · External exposure

The first step outside the school. When you bring in a guest speaker or trainer, you are using university resources and inviting someone in on the school's behalf, so there is an approval process with lead time.

Your plan covers

  • Who the speaker or trainer is and why they fit the group's mission
  • Date, format, audience, and room
  • How you'll promote it
  • Who hosts and handles communication with the guest

Checks

  • The faculty advisor submits the request through ZAG Activities
  • Administration evaluates the speaker, then the dean approves or declines
  • Nothing is confirmed with the guest until that approval comes back
  • Submit the plan to the advisor at least one to two weeks before the event. A request the week of the event won't work.

Tier 3 · External business

Doing work for an outside organization. This tier carries the most risk and the most responsibility, for the school, for the client, and for the students whose names are on the work.

Your plan covers

  • The client, and why the project fits the group's mission
  • Scope: the problem, the deliverables, and what's out of scope
  • The team, and why they're ready for this work
  • One named point of contact and a meeting schedule
  • Timeline with a midpoint and a final

Checks

  • Students may find, interview, and recommend clients. The advisor approves before anything is confirmed.
  • The advisor is aware of every engagement and copied on client email
  • The advisor is invited to kickoff, midpoint, and final meetings. Attendance isn't required; the invitation is.
  • A midpoint check-in catches problems early
  • The advisor reviews every deliverable before the client sees it
  • Closeout notes record what was delivered and what the team learned
NDAs and agreements. No student signs an NDA, contract, or agreement for the group. Any request for one goes to the advisor first and then to university counsel. Confidential client material stays in university-owned storage.

Roles and standards

No student group in the School of Business is entirely student-driven. These roles and standards hold at every tier.

Students

Lead and do the work

Student leaders run the group day to day.

  • Plan activities and submit plans before acting
  • Draft outreach, posts, and deliverables
  • Recommend clients and speakers
  • Keep the advisor updated on a regular schedule, without being asked
  • Represent the school professionally

Students are not agents of the university. They can't sign for it or be named as responsible parties.

Faculty advisor

Final say on external work

The university employee accountable for the group.

  • Approves plans, clients, and scopes
  • Submits ZAG Activities requests for speakers
  • Reviews deliverables before they reach a client
  • Routes NDAs and agreements to counsel
  • Connects the group to school resources and partners

The advisor's job is the approvals and the judgment calls. Students do the work.

School of Business

Owns the name and the resources

Administration, the dean's office, and marketing.

  • The dean approves outside speakers
  • The marketing coordinator oversees branded communication
  • Sets the requirements every student group follows
  • Provides rooms, systems, funding, and promotion

The group uses the school's name and resources, so the school sets the standards for using them.

Standards for every tier

Social media and promotionRun through and branded with the School of Business.
  • The advisor has access to every group account
  • A posting calendar each semester, shared with the advisor and the marketing coordinator
  • Posts that use Gonzaga branding or identify the group with the university are signed off by a university employee before they go out
  • No client work is posted without the client's and the advisor's approval
Email and outreachWho sends, from what account, and what it says.
  • Send from a group or Gonzaga account, never a personal one
  • A short list of approved senders
  • Standard templates for outreach to clients, alumni, and speakers, approved once by the advisor
  • The advisor is copied on client and partner threads
  • No commitments by email: dates, deliverables, or terms are confirmed only after approval
Name and logoThe Gonzaga and School of Business names belong to the school.
  • Use university branding only as the School of Business allows
  • Materials that carry the Gonzaga name are reviewed before release
  • Group names that reference Gonzaga or the School of Business stay with the school
University resourcesRooms, systems, funding, and approval processes.
  • Request rooms and resources through school channels
  • Use the ZAG Activities process for outside speakers
  • Plan for lead time: at least one to two weeks for outside speakers
Information and filesWhere work lives and who can see it.
  • Client files live in university-owned storage, not personal drives
  • Confidential client material is shared only with the project team and the advisor
  • When a student leaves or a project ends, access is removed and files stay with the group
  • Accounts and passwords belong to the group, not to individuals
Updates and recordsThe advisor stays informed without having to ask.
  • A short regular update to the advisor on all active work
  • Closeout notes for every event and project
  • Records carry forward so each new leadership team starts with what the last one learned
